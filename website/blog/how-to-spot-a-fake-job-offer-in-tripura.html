How to spot a fake job offer in Tripura

Fake appointment letters in the name of real departments are circulating in Tripura right now.

job scamfraudsafetyTripura

In December 2025, the Tripura State Electricity Corporation had to publicly warn job seekers that fraudsters were handing out fake appointment letters in its name. People were being contacted by phone and on WhatsApp by men claiming to be representatives of government departments, and were paying large sums for a government job that did not exist.

That is not an isolated case. A Tripura University employee has been arrested in a job scam. The Airports Authority of India went to the police over a fake job advertisement printed in an Agartala newspaper. Job aspirants detained a TSR jawan over false assurances of appointment.

The people being targeted are not foolish. They are qualified, they have been waiting a long time, and someone has offered them exactly what they have been working for.

How the racket works

It almost always follows the same shape.

Someone contacts you — a phone call, a WhatsApp message, or a friend of a friend who "knows someone in the department". They know your name, your qualification, sometimes which exams you have sat. They tell you a post is available, or that a list is being prepared, and that they can get your name on it.

Then comes the money. Not all at once. A small amount first, "for the file" or "for processing". Then a larger one when the appointment letter is ready. By the time the letter arrives and turns out to be worthless, you have paid several times and you are too embarrassed to tell anyone.

The five signs

1. Somebody asks you for money.

This is the whole thing. A genuine recruitment charges a published application fee, paid to the recruiting body through the official portal, with a receipt. Nobody asks you for cash, nobody asks you for a UPI transfer, and nobody asks for money to "secure" or "confirm" a post. If money is being asked for outside the official application fee, it is a fraud. There is no exception to this.

2. You are offered a post you never applied for.

Government recruitment runs on advertisements, applications, examinations and merit lists. Nobody is appointed because their name came up in conversation. An offer that arrives without an application behind it did not come from the department.

3. It arrives on WhatsApp.

TSECL's own advice was to rely only on official notifications. Real recruitments are announced in newspapers and on the department's own website. An appointment letter as a forwarded image is not a document; it is a picture of one.

4. There is urgency.

"The list closes tomorrow." "Only two seats left." Real recruitment runs to published dates that do not move because you were slow to pay. Urgency exists to stop you checking.

5. A middleman.

Agents, brokers, someone's cousin in the department. TSECL asked aspirants directly to avoid anyone claiming influence over appointments. Influence over a government appointment either does not exist, or it is a crime — and either way the person offering it is not helping you.

How to check before you pay anything

  • Go to the department's own website and look for the notification. Not a link someone sent you — type the address yourself.
  • Check the newspaper. Genuine recruitments are advertised.
  • Ring the department on the number published on its official site, not the number in the message.
  • Ask what the application fee is and where it is paid. The answer should be a published figure paid through the official portal.
  • Show the letter to someone. Fraud survives on embarrassment. Five minutes of a second opinion is cheaper than a year's salary.
If somebody is pressing you to decide today, that pressure is the product. Nothing honest needs it.

If you have already paid

You have not done anything stupid, and you are not alone. What matters now is speed.

  1. Stop paying. Not one more rupee, whatever stage they say it is at.
  2. Keep everything. Screenshots of the chats, the call log, the receipt or transfer reference, the letter, the bank statement. Do not delete the conversation out of anger.
  3. Report it to the police. Cyber fraud can be reported on the national cybercrime helpline 1930 and at cybercrime.gov.in, as well as at your local police station. The sooner a transfer is reported, the better the chance of stopping the money.
  4. Tell your bank, with the transaction reference.
  5. Tell the department being impersonated. TSECL went public precisely because aspirants reported it. Your complaint is what protects the next person.
  6. Tell other people. The racket runs on silence.

The only thing a job scam needs is for a qualified person to be tired of waiting. That describes most people reading this. Knowing the shape of it is most of the defence.
