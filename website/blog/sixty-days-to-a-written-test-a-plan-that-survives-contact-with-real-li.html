Sixty days to a written test: a plan that survives contact with real life

Most study plans fail in the first week because they assume perfect days. This one assumes you will miss some, and still gets you to the exam prepared.

preparationmock testsstudy planwritten exam

The notification comes out. You download the syllabus, and it is four pages long. You make a timetable with eight hours a day on it. You keep to it for four days, miss a day because of something at home, and then the timetable is dead because you are already behind.

This happens to almost everyone. The problem is not discipline. The problem is a plan with no room in it.

Start from the paper, not the syllabus

The syllabus tells you what could be asked. The previous papers tell you what is asked, and in what proportion. Those are different things, and the second one is far more useful.

Before you study anything, sit with two or three previous papers for the same post and count. How many questions came from each section? Which topics turn up every single year?

You will usually find that a small number of topics carry most of the marks. That is where your first thirty days go.

The shape of the sixty days

Days 1 to 30 — the heavy topics. Take the three or four sections that carry the most marks. One topic at a time, finished before you start the next. Finished means you can answer questions on it, not that you have read it.

Days 31 to 45 — everything else. The smaller sections, covered more quickly. You are not trying to master these; you are trying not to lose easy marks.

Days 46 to 55 — full papers under time. One complete paper, timed, with no phone. Then two hours going through what you got wrong. The second part matters more than the first.

Days 56 to 60 — only revision. No new topics. Your own notes, your own mistakes. Nothing you have not seen before.

Build the missed days in

Here is the part most plans leave out. Plan five days a week, not seven.

The other two days are not rest days. They are catch-up days. When something comes up — and it will — you have somewhere to put the work instead of falling behind. On a week when nothing goes wrong, you get ahead or you revise.

A plan you keep to for sixty days at five days a week beats a plan you abandon in week two.

Keep a mistakes notebook

One page per topic. Every question you got wrong goes in it, with one line on why.

Not the correct answer — the reason. "Confused the two amendments." "Read the question too fast." "Did not know the formula."

By week six this notebook is the single most valuable thing you own, because it is a list of exactly where your marks are leaking. In the last five days, it is the only thing you need to read.

Take the test before you are ready

People put off the first mock test until they feel prepared. That is backwards. Take one in the first week, when you will do badly, because a bad score in week one is information and a bad score in week eight is a disaster.

It tells you where you actually stand, how fast you need to read, and which sections you were wrong to feel confident about.

On the day before

Do not study anything new. Read your mistakes notebook, check that your admit card, photo identification and pens are in one place, and sleep.

Every year people lose marks they had earned because they sat up until two in the morning learning something that was never going to be asked.

If sixty days is not what you have

The shape still works, compressed. Half the time on the heavy topics, a quarter on everything else, a quarter on full papers and revision. What does not change is the mistakes notebook and the catch-up days.

And if the exam is months away rather than weeks, the honest answer is that the first thirty days above are the whole game. Most people never finish them.
