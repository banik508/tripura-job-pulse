How to write a CV for a Tripura government job

A government CV is read differently from a private one. Here is what to put in, what to leave out, and the four mistakes that get a good candidate passed over.

CVgovernment jobsapplicationsTripura

Most advice about CVs is written for private sector hiring, where a recruiter spends six seconds deciding whether to read on. A government recruitment works differently. Your CV is usually read after you have already cleared a written test, by someone checking whether you are who your application said you were.

That changes what belongs in it.

Start with what they have to verify

The person reading has a list. Your name as it appears on your certificates. Your date of birth. Your qualification and the year you passed. Your category, if you have claimed one. Your contact details.

Put all of this at the top, in plain text, where it can be found without hunting. Not in a sidebar, not in a coloured box, not spread across a two-column layout. If the person has to search for your date of birth, you have made their job harder on the one day you needed it to be easy.

  • Full name, spelt exactly as on your certificates
  • Father's or mother's name, if the form asked for it
  • Date of birth
  • Mobile number and an email address you actually check
  • Present address and permanent address

Use the same spelling everywhere. If your certificate says Debbarma and your CV says Debbarma, your application form had better not say Devbarma. Small differences become big problems at the verification stage.

Education goes before experience

In a private CV, experience comes first. In a government one, the qualification is usually the eligibility condition, so it goes first.

For each qualification give the exam or degree, the board or university, the year you passed, and your marks or percentage. If a result is awaited, say so plainly rather than leaving a gap.

A gap you do not explain gets read as a gap you are hiding. A gap you explain in one line stops being interesting.

Say what you did, not where you sat

This is where almost every CV goes wrong. Compare these two lines:

  • Responsible for office work and other duties as assigned.
  • Handled 40 files a day, prepared the monthly report in Excel, and trained five new staff.

Both describe the same job. The second one tells the reader you can do something. Numbers help because they are specific — how many files, how many people, how many months, what percentage.

If your experience is a part-time job, a family business, a tuition class or volunteer work, it still counts. Write it down. "Taught 15 students for Madhyamik for two years" is real experience and it says something true about you.

Skills, written honestly

List the things you can actually demonstrate. Typing speed, if you have measured it. Specific software, not "computer knowledge". Languages you can read, write and speak, kept separate — many people in Tripura can speak Kokborok fluently but would not claim to write it formally, and saying so is better than being found out.

If you are not sure of your typing speed, measure it rather than guessing. There is a typing test on this site that will tell you in two minutes.

The four mistakes

  1. Three pages. One page if you are a fresher, two at the most. Nobody is impressed by length.
  2. A photograph stuck in the corner at the wrong size. If the form asks for a photo, attach it as the form asks. Do not embed it in the CV unless told to.
  3. An email address you made in school. Make a plain one with your name in it.
  4. One CV sent everywhere. The vacancy names the qualification and the skills it wants. Those exact words should appear in your CV, where they are true of you.

Before you send it

Read it out loud. You will hear the mistakes you cannot see.
